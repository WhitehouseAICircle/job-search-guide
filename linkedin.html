Refresh your LinkedIn profile

If you use LinkedIn, a chat called LinkedIn profile refresh can help bring your profile up to date alongside your CV. Work through one section at a time, using your real experience. If you do not use LinkedIn, skip this optional guide.

This is a writing exercise with fictional examples. It does not connect to your LinkedIn account, change your settings or publish anything for you. The prompts have not been tested in a live LinkedIn profile-editing session.

1. Check sharing before editing

If you are employed and want to avoid announcing changes, check LinkedIn's controls before saving edits:

  1. On desktop, open Me → Settings & Privacy → Visibility.
  2. Under Visibility of your LinkedIn activity, open Share profile updates and switch it Off. On mobile, start from your profile photo and Settings & Privacy, then follow the Visibility settings. Labels may vary.
  3. While editing, also check any Share with network or similar toggle and leave it off if you do not want an announcement.

This controls sharing of certain profile updates, such as job or education changes and work anniversaries. It does not hide your profile edits. People who visit your profile, including colleagues or an employer, can still see saved changes. It is not a guarantee of a private job search. See LinkedIn's sharing guidance.

Open to Work is a separate choice. Sharing it with all members includes people at your current company and adds a profile-photo frame. Recruiters-only visibility is more limited; LinkedIn takes steps to exclude recruiters at your current company but cannot guarantee complete privacy. Check the current employer on your profile is accurate. You do not need to enable Open to Work to improve your profile. Read LinkedIn's Open to Work guidance before choosing.

2. Give ChatGPT a small, safe starting point

Start LinkedIn profile refresh inside your job-search Project. Use the fictional Candidate A pack first. For your own profile, paste only the sections you want help with and a short checked evidence list. A profile link alone may be inaccessible or show only part of the page; do not assume ChatGPT has read it.

Remove phone numbers, email addresses, exact home addresses, colleagues' names, private recruiter messages and confidential workplace details. Describe employers or clients generically where their identity is unnecessary. You do not need to upload a full profile export or disclose your account password. See the privacy notes.

Help me refresh my LinkedIn profile in UK English, one section at a time.
Use only the profile text and checked evidence I supply next.
Ask which type of role I want to be considered for and what tone I prefer.
Treat my career goals as goals, not experience I already have.

Review my headline, About section, experience, skills, education
and any other sections I choose to supply. First give me a short
priority list: outdated detail, unclear wording, missing evidence,
or useful information to consider adding. Ask before drafting.

Do not invent qualifications, job titles, dates, achievements,
numbers, endorsements or software expertise. Mark uncertain facts
as [confirm] and ask one question at a time where needed.
Do not use personal memories or connected sources for this exercise.
Prepare drafts only. Do not access my account or publish changes.

For a first practice, add: “Use only Candidate A's fictional evidence from the walkthrough. Ask me to paste it if it is not available. No LinkedIn profile text has been supplied, so do not claim to have audited an existing profile.”

3. Refresh the headline and About section

Your headline is the short introduction below your name; it is different from a job title in Experience. Your About section is your longer summary, not a second separate summary section to fill in.

Review an old headline for relevance. You can explain useful strengths or a career direction without claiming you already hold your target job. Keep actual employment titles and dates accurate; do not turn a hoped-for promotion into a current position.

Using my checked evidence, suggest three plain-English headlines:
one focused on my current work, one on transferable skills, and
one on my stated career direction. Avoid exaggerated titles,
keyword stuffing and claims that I already hold my target role.
Explain the evidence behind each option.

Then draft an About section of around 120–180 words covering
my experience, two evidenced strengths and the work I want to develop.
Keep it natural. Do not announce that I am job hunting unless I ask.
Flag anything I need to confirm outside the draft.

Fictional headline example: “Retail assistant | Customer service, organisation and basic spreadsheet experience”. For Candidate A, “Office manager | Advanced Excel specialist” would add unsupported claims. A public career-direction headline may itself signal interest in a change, even with update sharing off.

4. Bring experience and skills up to date

Check your current role, employment dates and responsibilities. Replace vague descriptions with clear examples of what you actually do. Include relevant volunteering, study or caring experience only where you choose to share it and can describe it accurately.

Review the Experience text and evidence I supply below.
Show a table: original wording, suggested wording, supporting evidence,
and anything to confirm. Suggest up to three concise bullets per role.
Keep official job titles, dates and employment status accurate.
Ask about outcomes rather than inventing numbers.
Remove confidential client, colleague or internal business details.

Suggest relevant skills supported by this evidence and explain why
each fits. Separate skills I can demonstrate now from skills to learn.
Do not add a skill just because it appears in a target job advert.

There is no need to fill the profile with as many skills as possible. Choose relevant skills you could explain in an interview. Candidate A can evidence customer service and basic spreadsheet use; diary management and advanced Excel remain unconfirmed. Keep future learning in a private development list until you have evidence.

5. Consider the other useful details

Use this as a review checklist, not a requirement to fill every section:

Area What to check
Education and qualifications Correct names, completion status and dates; distinguish studying from completed
Licences and certifications Accurate issuing body, validity and expiry where applicable; never invent a credential
Projects, volunteering and languages Relevant, current examples and an honest description of your contribution or ability
Featured work or portfolio Public work you own or have permission to share; remove confidential data and check links
Photo and banner Optional images you are comfortable making visible and have permission to use
Location and contact details Enough information for your purpose without unnecessary personal contact or home details
Recommendations Request genuine feedback yourself if useful; never fabricate someone else's endorsement

Ask: “Which of these areas would strengthen my profile based on the evidence I have supplied? Give me three priorities, not a list of things to invent.”

6. Review, then update it yourself

  • Save your existing wording privately before replacing it.
  • Compare the drafts with your CV: roles, dates, qualifications and claims should agree. Your profile can be broader than a CV tailored to one vacancy.
  • Read it aloud and remove wording that does not sound like you or that you could not explain in an interview.
  • Recheck sharing controls, then copy approved wording into LinkedIn one section at a time. Review how the saved profile looks and any visibility options you use.
  • Check links and proofread. Do not assume switching sharing off afterwards will undo an announcement already sent.

Keep your checked evidence summary for CV tailoring and Job Sweep. You can use a relevant advert to understand employers' language, while keeping only skills and experience you actually have. No profile refresh can guarantee recruiter interest or a job offer.
